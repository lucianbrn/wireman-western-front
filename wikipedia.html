Ralph Tjarks Brucker (May 8, 1917 – September 20, 1991) was an American farmer and soldier from Illinois who, according to family accounts, served in the United States Army in the European Theater of World War II as a wireman — a field lineman who laid and repaired the telephone wire that linked front-line units to command posts. The family account holds that he rose to the rank of Technical Sergeant and received four bronze service stars for the Normandy, Northern France, Rhineland, and Ardennes-Alsace campaigns. His unit assignment has not been identified, and his rank, role, and campaigns have not been confirmed against primary military records; they are presented here as the family preserved them.[3]

Brucker spent most of his life in Ford and McLean Counties, Illinois, before joining the postwar migration to Southern California, where he died in Ontario in 1991. In 2026 his great-grandson built an interactive museum exhibit, Wireman of the Western Front, around the surviving record of his life.[4]

Early life [edit]

Brucker was born on May 8, 1917, in Illinois.[1] Family chronology places his birth at Cropsey, in McLean County, while his 1940 draft registration gives his birthplace as Ford, Illinois; the discrepancy is preserved in the family archive rather than resolved.[2] He was a son of William Clarence Brucker (1890–1959) and Mary G. Brucker (1892–1981), whose maiden name, Tjarks, he carried as his middle name. He was one of at least ten children; his siblings included Marie, Wilfred, Henry William, Robert, Edith, Donald Harry, Violet, and Martin Dale Brucker.[2] A baptism for him was recorded at Anchor, Illinois, on June 25, 1922.[2]

By 1940 he was living at Cropsey, in Ford County, and working as a self-employed farmer.[5]

Marriage and family [edit]

Brucker married Annabel Elliott (1921–2011) at Cropsey in 1941.[2] The couple had four children: Audrey (born c. 1942), Stephen Elliott (born June 1, 1944), Loren (born c. 1947), and Mary (born 1948).[2] Annabel survived him by twenty years, dying at Ontario, California, on April 4, 2011.[6]

World War II [edit]

Draft registration

Brucker registered for the draft on October 16, 1940, in Illinois, in the first peacetime registration under the Selective Training and Service Act.[5] His registration card describes a 23-year-old self-employed man, 6 feet tall and 190 pounds, with a light complexion, brown hair, and hazel eyes, and names his father, William C. Brucker, as his next of kin.[5] The card records his middle name as “Jarks,” a spelling variant preserved in the family archive's transcription. Registration alone is not evidence of enlistment or service; an enlistment-index entry for a Ralph T. Brucker, serial number 36027430, at Chicago in 1941, is a research lead only and has not been shown to be his.[3]

Service account

A family photograph of Brucker's circle in service; the men in it have not all been identified.

According to the account preserved by his family, Brucker served in the U.S. Army in the European Theater as a wireman, attained the rank of Technical Sergeant — the second-highest enlisted grade in the wartime Army, typically held by a platoon sergeant or section chief — and received four bronze service stars.[3] Service stars are campaign-participation credits worn on the ribbon of the European-African-Middle Eastern Campaign Medal, one for each designated campaign in which a soldier's unit participated; they are distinct from the Bronze Star Medal, an individual decoration.[7][8] The four campaigns named in the family account, with their official dates, are:

  • Normandy — June 6 to July 24, 1944
  • Northern France — July 25 to September 14, 1944
  • Rhineland — September 15, 1944, to March 21, 1945
  • Ardennes-Alsace — December 16, 1944, to January 25, 1945 (the Battle of the Bulge)

The account is internally consistent: a soldier who came ashore in the Normandy campaign and served continuously through the Bulge would earn exactly these four stars. It also raises a question the family has not answered: continuous service through VE Day would ordinarily have added a fifth star, for Central Europe (March 22 to May 11, 1945). If the family's count of four is exact, Brucker may have left the theater before late March 1945 — by wounds, rotation, or reassignment — but no record has been found to say so.[3]

The wireman's trade

The wartime Army specialty of field lineman (Military Occupational Specialty 641) belonged to the Signal Corps. Wire crews laid field telephone wire from reel trucks, spliced lines cut by shellfire, strung wire across rivers and through shelled towns, and kept the circuits alive that carried orders and artillery calls; at the height of the Battle of the Bulge, Third Army signalmen maintained a network built from some 20,000 miles of field wire.[9] A Technical Sergeant in such a unit would typically have led a wire section. That Brucker held this specialty rests on the family account; no surviving document in the family's possession names his military occupation.[3]

Later life and death [edit]

Brucker in later life (family photograph).

After the war Brucker returned to Illinois. The family chronology places him at Belle Prairie, in Livingston County, in 1950, and at San Bernardino, California, by 1964 — part of the large postwar movement of Midwestern families to Southern California.[2] He died at Ontario, in San Bernardino County, on September 20, 1991, at the age of 74, and was buried at Ontario.[1][2]

Unresolved questions [edit]

Research into Brucker's service continues. The records that would settle the open points include his discharge papers (held, if anywhere, by the family), his Official Military Personnel File at the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis (subject to the losses of the 1973 fire),[10] the free World War II enlistment index at the National Archives, his September 1991 obituary in the Inland Valley Daily Bulletin, and any Veterans Administration headstone application. Until one surfaces, his unit, and the confirmation of his rank and campaigns, remain open questions.[3]

Legacy [edit]

In October 2026, Brucker's great-grandson Lucian Brucker built Ralph Tjarks Brucker: Wireman of the Western Front, a fourteen-frame interactive museum exhibit — part film, part research archive — presenting the four surviving family photographs, the 1940 draft record, the family service account labeled as such, and official photographs of the four campaigns and the wireman's craft. The exhibit keeps a public list of what remains unknown about his service.[4]
