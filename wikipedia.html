Ralph Tjarks Brucker (May 8, 1917 – September 20, 1991) was an American soldier, farmer, and electrician from Illinois who served in the United States Army in World War II and was awarded the Bronze Star Medal for heroism in action. Inducted in the peacetime draft of 1941 — his enlistment record lists his civilian occupation as “Electricians”[7] — he trained at camps in South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Louisiana, and Florida[26] before serving in the European Theater with the 28th Infantry Division. In December 1944 his hometown newspapers reported that the division commander, Major General Norman D. Cota, had awarded him the Bronze Star for heroism against the enemy in France.[21][22] His 1991 obituary credits him with four bronze battle stars, for the Normandy, Northern France, Rhineland, and Ardennes campaigns.[8] The account preserved by his family holds that he worked as a wireman — a field lineman who laid and repaired the telephone wire that linked front-line units to command posts;[12] his specific company and his military occupation have not been confirmed against primary service records, and this article distinguishes throughout between the documentary record and the family account.

After the war Brucker farmed in Illinois before moving to Ontario, California, in the late 1950s, where he worked as an electrician at Lanterman State Hospital and died in 1991.[8] In 2026 his great-grandson built an interactive museum exhibit, Wireman of the Western Front, around the surviving record of his life.[19]

Early life and family background [edit]

Brucker was born on May 8, 1917, at Cropsey, in McLean County, Illinois, a son of William Clarence Brucker (1890–1959) and Mary G. Brucker (1892–1981).[5][8] His 1940 draft registration instead gives his birthplace as Ford, Illinois; the discrepancy is preserved rather than resolved.[6] He was baptized on June 25, 1922, at St. John's Lutheran church in Anchor, Illinois.[3] He was one of at least ten children; his siblings included Marie, Wilfred, Henry William, Robert, Edith, Donald, Violet, and Martin Dale Brucker.[4][8]

Brucker grew up on the family's farms in Ford County. The 1920 census shows the household in Sullivant Township;[4] the 1930 census places them on Becker Road, where Ralph, aged 13, attended school;[4] and in 1940, aged 22 and a high-school graduate, he was living with his parents and working on his own account.[4]

His middle name was his mother's maiden name. Mary G. Tjarks was a daughter of Gerhard Theodore Tjarks (1862–1952), who emigrated from Ostfriesland, in northwestern Germany, in 1888.[5] The Brucker line reaches back through Ralph's grandfather Henry Brucker (1864–1938) to Gottlieb Friedrich Brucker (1832–1908), born at Denkendorf in the Kingdom of Württemberg, who emigrated to Illinois in 1850.[5]

Marriage and family [edit]

Brucker married Annabel Elliott (1921–2011) on July 21, 1941, three months after his induction into the Army — not in Illinois, as later family trees assumed, but at Spartanburg, South Carolina, near Camp Croft, where he was training, at the home of a Judge Pace; the marriage was announced in the Sibley and Fairbury newspapers.[27] The couple's children were Audrey Ann (born November 1942, at Fairbury hospital), Stephen Elliott (born June 1, 1944), Loren (born December 25, 1946), and Mary (born December 22, 1947); his obituary additionally names a son, Bob Beck of Ontario, California.[8][28] Audrey married Paul G. Dryden in Nevada in 1965.[5] Annabel survived her husband by twenty years, dying at Ontario on April 4, 2011.[20]

World War II [edit]

Draft registration

Brucker registered for the draft on October 16, 1940, in Illinois, in the first peacetime registration under the Selective Training and Service Act.[6] His registration card describes a 23-year-old self-employed man, 6 feet tall and 190 pounds, with brown hair and hazel eyes, and names his father, William C. Brucker, as his next of kin.[6] The card records his middle name under the spelling “Jarks.”[6]

Enlistment

Brucker was inducted into the Army on April 21, 1941, at Chicago.[7] His enlistment record, preserved in the National Archives' Electronic Army Serial Number Merged File, lists him as a Private and a Selectee — a draftee under the Selective Service system — resident in Ford County, with four years of high school, a height of 5 feet 11 inches, and a weight of 190 pounds.[7] It gives his service number as 36027430 and his civilian occupation as “Electricians.”[7] He enlisted as a single man without dependents; he married that July.[5][7] His hometown paper, the Fairbury Blade, reported his departure for the Army that same April.[26]

Training

Brucker's stateside service was followed closely by his hometown newspapers.[26] He trained first at Camp Croft, South Carolina; in July 1941 he was reported stationed at Fort Bragg, North Carolina, where Annabel traveled to visit him;[33] and in August 1941 he was transferred to Indiantown Gap, Pennsylvania, coming home on a seven-day furlough; that Christmas, home from Indiantown Gap, he was the guest of honor at a dinner reported by the Paxton Record.[26] By early 1942 he was at Camp Livingston, Louisiana; Annabel traveled to Alexandria, Louisiana, to visit him, and later stayed near the camp.[26] The hometown reports promoted him in print from Private to Private First Class (May 1942) and to Corporal (February 1943).[26] In May 1943 he was reported home on furlough from Camp Gordon Johnston, Florida.[26] When he joined the 28th Infantry Division is not covered by the surviving reports.

Service account

A family photograph of Brucker's circle in service, Germany; the men in it have not all been identified.

Brucker served in the European Theater with the 28th Infantry Division.[21][22] On December 28, 1944, the Paxton Record announced on its front page, under the headline “Awarded Medal For Heroism in Action,” that the division's commander, Major General Norman D. Cota, had awarded the Bronze Star Medal for heroism against the enemy to Technician Fourth Grade Ralph T. Brucker; the Fairbury Blade carried the award on its own front page on January 12, 1945.[21][22] In June 1944 The Pantagraph reported his promotion from Corporal to Sergeant, on word from the War Department.[29] In March 1945 the Blade described him in a combat narrative, with his unit in the snow in France,[23] and ten days later The Pantagraph carried its own combat anecdote about him, naming the 28th Infantry Division in print.[30] His obituary, published in The Pantagraph on September 24, 1991, additionally records four bronze battle stars, for Normandy, Northern France, Rhineland, and Ardennes — the Battle of the Bulge.[8] Service stars are campaign-participation credits worn on the ribbon of the European-African-Middle Eastern Campaign Medal, one for each designated campaign in which a soldier's unit participated; they are distinct from the Bronze Star Medal, an individual decoration.[15][16] The four campaigns, with their official dates, are:

CampaignOfficial datesNotes
NormandyJune 6 – July 24, 1944The Allied invasion of Normandy and the fighting in the hedgerows.
Northern FranceJuly 25 – September 14, 1944The breakout from Normandy and the pursuit across France; the liberation of Paris (August 25) falls within this campaign.
RhinelandSeptember 15, 1944 – March 21, 1945The advance to the Rhine; the Hürtgen Forest fighting of autumn 1944 falls within this campaign.
Ardennes-AlsaceDecember 16, 1944 – January 25, 1945The Battle of the Bulge, named “Ardennes” in Brucker's obituary.[8]

The family account adds what the records do not: that Brucker served as a wireman, leading men who strung communication wire.[12] In a 2017 interview in his school newspaper, Brucker's grandson Ryan Brucker, a history teacher in Irvine, California, recalled that his grandfather fought at Normandy and the Bulge and in the liberation of Paris, and on one occasion captured, and took prisoner, the German soldiers who had thrown a grenade at him.[13] Ryan also said a bronze star hung with his grandfather's jacket — an account the 1944 award reports have since borne out.[13][21] The 28th Division's record fits the four campaigns of the obituary: it landed in Normandy in late July 1944, paraded through Paris on August 29, fought in the Hürtgen Forest, and held a sector struck in the Ardennes offensive. No roster naming Brucker has yet been found, and his company within the division remains unknown.

One question survives the new record: continuous service through VE Day would ordinarily have added a fifth service star, for Central Europe (March 22 to May 11, 1945), yet his obituary counts four.[8] He remained in service through the end of the war in Europe and was discharged in June 1945 (see below); whether his unit's Central Europe participation was credited to him personally is not shown by any record found so far.

AwardBasis

Bronze Star MedalAwarded for heroism in action against the enemy in France by Maj. Gen. Norman D. Cota, commanding the 28th Infantry Division; announced by the Paxton Record (front page, December 28, 1944) and the Fairbury Blade (front page, January 12, 1945).[21][22]

The insignia shown are the standard issue of his documented division, grade, and award. Brucker's own Bronze Star and Army jacket survive in his grandson Ryan's classroom (see Commemoration and legacy).

The wireman's trade

The wartime Army specialty of field lineman (Military Occupational Specialty 641) belonged to the Signal Corps. Wire crews laid field telephone wire from reel trucks, spliced lines cut by shellfire, strung wire across rivers and through shelled towns, and kept the circuits alive that carried orders and artillery calls; at the height of the Battle of the Bulge, Third Army signalmen maintained a network built from some 20,000 miles of field wire.[17] A senior technician in such a unit would typically have led a wire section. That Brucker held this specialty rests on the family account;[12] but it fits the man the record describes — the Army had inducted a working electrician.[7]

Discharge and homecoming

Brucker was discharged in June 1945 and arrived home near Cropsey on Monday, June 18; the Fairbury Blade reported that he had been in Germany.[24] On June 29 the Cropsey community gathered in the club rooms to welcome him home — the first returned overseas veteran of the community, with about 75 neighbors present.[25] That September he gave a talk about his service, in the States and overseas, and in October he and Annabel bought the E. W. Convis property.[25][28]

Later life and death [edit]

Brucker in later life (family photograph).

After the war Brucker returned to Illinois farming. In April 1946 the Blade credited him with stopping a runaway tractor whose driver had been knocked unconscious; the injured man reached Fairbury hospital alive.[28] In November 1951 he lost three fingers of his right hand in a corn picker on his father-in-law's farm; neighbors harvested his forty acres while he recovered, and his thank-you cards were published in the Blade.[31] The 1950 census records him at Belle Prairie, in Livingston County, farming on his own account and working 75 hours a week, with Annabel and the children.[4] In June 1957 he advertised his farm equipment and household effects for sale in The Pantagraph — the liquidation that preceded the move.[32] The family then joined the postwar migration to Southern California; his obituary describes him as a resident of Ontario for 34 years at his death.[8] In 1964 he registered to vote from 909 East Harvard Place, Ontario.[11] He worked as an electrician at Lanterman State Hospital in Pomona.[8]

Brucker died at his home in Ontario on September 20, 1991, at the age of 74.[2][8] His obituary identifies him as a member of the United Brethren Church at Saybrook, Illinois.[9] Services were held by Draper Mortuary of Ontario, with entombment in Bellevue Mausoleum;[8] his burial is recorded at Bellevue Memorial Park in Ontario, where he shares a bronze headstone, bearing a ceramic portrait of the couple, with Annabel.[10]

Unresolved questions [edit]

Research into Brucker's service continues, and several points remain open:

  • Company. His division is documented, but his company and regiment within the 28th Infantry Division are not. His Army jacket — which survives, hanging in his grandson Ryan's classroom in Irvine[13] — and the division's morning reports are the most promising routes.
  • The wireman's job. His military occupation still rests on the family account.[12] His discharge papers and his personnel file at the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis, subject to the losses of the 1973 fire,[18] could confirm it.
  • The March 1945 combat story. Only a fragment of the Fairbury Blade's March 2, 1945 narrative[23] is readable in the free newspaper archive; its full text may describe the grenade fight his grandson recounts.[13]
  • Final grade. The hometown papers document a promotion from Corporal to Sergeant in June 1944,[29] while the award reports that December style him Technician Fourth Grade (T/4)[21] — a technician grade of equivalent standing; the family remembers Technical Sergeant.[12] His discharge paper would state his final grade exactly.
  • The fifth star. Whether the Central Europe campaign was credited to him, which would have added a fifth service star, is unknown (see Service account).
  • A caution on records. A Veterans Affairs BIRLS death-file entry for a “Ralph W. Bracker” (born January 13, 1917; died July 10, 1991) appears attached to Brucker's name in online trees. It belongs to a different man and is reproduced in error.
  • Two unexplained births. The Fairbury Blade reported a daughter born to Mr. and Mrs. Ralph Brucker on November 14, 1952, and a child born June 1, 1954.[28] Neither matches the documented children above nor the survivors named in his obituary; they may record children who died young, or items misattributed in print. They are noted here so the record stays honest, and are not counted among his children in this article.
  • “Wayne.” The 1950 census lists a son Wayne, aged 3, in the household where the documented children have Loren (born December 25, 1946)[4][28] — the ages fit exactly; possibly the same child under another name.

Commemoration and legacy [edit]

The National World War II Museum in New Orleans lists a commemorative brick purchased in Brucker's honor in its Road to Victory brick program: “RALPH T BRUCKER US ARMY INFANTRY NORMANDY INVASION.”[14] His Army jacket hangs on the wall of his grandson Ryan Brucker's classroom at Woodbridge High School in Irvine, California, where Ryan teaches history; Annabel gave it to him, and Ryan has said his Bronze Star hangs with it.[13]

In October 2026, Brucker's great-grandson Lucian Brucker built Ralph Tjarks Brucker: Wireman of the Western Front, a fourteen-frame interactive museum exhibit — part film, part research archive — presenting the surviving family photographs, the draft and enlistment records, the family service account labeled as such, and official photographs of the four campaigns and the wireman's craft. The exhibit keeps a public list of what remains unknown about his service.[19]
